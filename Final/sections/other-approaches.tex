\section{Other Approaches}
\label{sec:other}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The techniques presented in the previous sections are a small part of a large
body of work. This section gives an overview of further approaches, with
pointers to the literature and to other chapters of this handbook. More
detailed surveys can be found in~\cite{AST18,BJKKRVW15,Esp14,ZP04}.

\subsection{Counting Abstraction and Well-Structured Models}

When processes are anonymous, a configuration can be represented by counting
the number of processes in each local state. This \emph{counting abstraction}
is exact, and it turns a system of finite-state processes that communicate
through rendez-vous or shared variables into a Petri net, or equivalently a
vector addition system. German and Sistla~\cite{GerSis92} considered systems
consisting of a control process and an arbitrary number of identical user
processes, communicating by rendez-vous. They gave decision procedures for
indexed temporal properties, which run in time polynomial in the size of the
process template when all processes are identical, and in doubly exponential
time in the presence of a control process. Safety properties reduce to the
coverability problem for Petri nets, which is
\textsc{ExpSpace}-complete~\cite{Rac78,Esp98} and can be solved by the backward
reachability analysis of Section~\ref{sec:wqo}. For liveness properties of the
control process, the property is first combined with the control process
through a B\"uchi automaton, and the existence of a fair run that visits
accepting states infinitely often is reduced to the reachability problem for
Petri nets, which is decidable~\cite{GerSis92}.

\emph{Broadcast protocols}~\cite{EFM99} extend Petri nets with transitions in
which all processes in a given state react to a message. They are
well-structured, so safety properties expressed by upward-closed sets are
decidable, while liveness properties become undecidable. Delzanno~\cite{Del00}
applied counting abstraction with symbolic representations based on linear
arithmetic to verify cache coherence protocols for an arbitrary number of
caches. These ideas have been extended to systems with pairwise rendez-vous on
various topologies~\cite{AKRSV18}, to asynchronous shared-memory systems in
which anonymous processes communicate through shared registers~\cite{EGM16}, to
population protocols~\cite{AADFP06,EGLM17}, and to ad hoc networks, in which
processes broadcast to their neighbors in an unknown graph~\cite{DSZ10}. A
recurring observation is that, when processes are anonymous and any behavior of
one process can be replicated by others, the complexity of parameterized
verification is often much lower than that of Petri net
coverability~\cite{Esp14}. The general theory of well-structured transition
systems, and the many models to which it applies, are the subject of
Chapter~\chapref{Well-Structured Systems}.

\subsection{Cutoffs}

A \emph{cutoff} for a class of systems and properties is a number~$k$ such
that a property holds for all instances of a system whenever it holds for all
instances of size at most~$k$. A cutoff theorem reduces parameterized
verification to finitely many finite-state model checking problems. An early
approach of this kind establishes a correspondence between systems of
different sizes that preserves an indexed version of CTL$^*$ without the
next-time operator~\cite{BCG89}. Emerson and Namjoshi~\cite{EN95} showed that,
in rings in which a single token without data is passed between the processes,
properties that refer to a fixed number of processes have a small cutoff that
is independent of the process template. The result was extended to other
topologies by decomposing networks into small fragments~\cite{CTTV04}, and the
existence of cutoffs for token-passing systems has been characterized in terms
of the topology and the specification logic~\cite{AJKR14}.

Emerson and Kahlon~\cite{EK00} considered \emph{guarded protocols}, in which a
transition is guarded by a \emph{conjunctive} condition, requiring all other
processes to be in given states, or by a \emph{disjunctive} condition,
requiring at least one of them to be.\lukas{maybe say that it is similar to our universally, existentially quantified tranitions?} They established cutoffs that grow
linearly with the number of local states of the template. For disjunctive
guards, the argument is that each guard needs only one witness, so a small
instance that keeps one process in each local state that occurs in a run can
simulate the run. For conjunctive guards, removing processes can only make the
guards easier to satisfy, so the projection of a run onto a subset of the
processes is a run of a smaller instance; liveness properties require more
care, since the projection of a fair run need not be fair. The cutoffs were
later sharpened and extended to fairness and deadlock
detection~\cite{AJK16}, and specialized cutoff methods were developed for cache
coherence protocols~\cite{EK03}. Static cutoffs may be large, and each theorem
covers a specific class of systems. Dynamic cutoff methods, such as view
abstraction (Section~\ref{sec:views}) and the method of Kaiser, Kroening, and
Wahl~\cite{KKW10} for programs with an unbounded number of threads, discover
the cutoff during the analysis.

\subsection{Abstraction and Network Invariants}

A \emph{network invariant} is a process~$I$ that abstracts the composition of
any number of copies of the template. Kurshan and McMillan~\cite{KM89}, and
independently Wolper and Lovinfosse~\cite{WL89}, observed that it suffices to
check that $I$ abstracts a single copy, and that the composition of~$I$ with
one more copy is again abstracted by~$I$; verifying the composition of~$I$
with a fixed environment then establishes the property for all sizes. The
approach was generalized to networks generated by grammars~\cite{CGJ97}, to
invariants given by automata for linear networks~\cite{SisGyu99}, and to
invariants that are computed automatically~\cite{LHR97} or established
deductively~\cite{KPSZ02}. Symmetry reduction exploits the fact that the
identity of an individual process is irrelevant, and explores equivalence
classes of configurations instead of configurations~\cite{EmeSis96,CEFJ96,ID96,GyuSis97};
it applies to each instance separately, but it is the basis of the counting
abstraction above.

Several abstractions summarize the processes by a finite amount of
information. \emph{$(0,1,\infty)$-counter abstraction}~\cite{PXZ02} records,
for each local state, whether zero, one, or many processes occupy it, and adds
fairness conditions derived from the concrete system so that liveness
properties can be proved. \emph{Environment abstraction}~\cite{CTV06,CTV08}
describes the system from the point of view of one reference process, by
predicates that relate the other processes to it. \emph{Indexed
predicates}~\cite{LB07} lift predicate abstraction to quantified invariants.
For cache coherence protocols, compositional model checking~\cite{McM01} and
the CMP method~\cite{CMP04} keep a few processes concrete and replace the others
by a single abstract process, whose behavior is constrained by noninterference
lemmas; many of these lemmas can be derived from the message flows of the
protocol~\cite{TT08}. For programs with an unbounded number of identical
threads, \emph{thread-modular} reasoning verifies one thread against an
assumption on the others~\cite{FQ03}, and its precision can be increased by
reasoning about several threads at a time~\cite{HMP17,FKP15}.\lukas{similar to view abstraction refinement}

\subsection{Deductive and SMT-Based Methods}

Deductive methods prove that a quantified formula is an inductive invariant,
that is, that it holds initially and is preserved by every transition, for an
arbitrary number of processes. The approach goes back to the proof method of
Owicki and Gries~\cite{OG76}, and is supported by languages and tools such as
TLA+~\cite{Lam94,Lam02,CDLMRV12}, which has been applied to industrial
protocols~\cite{NRZBMD15}. The main difficulty is to find the invariant. The
\emph{invisible invariants} method~\cite{PRZ01,APRXZ01} computes the reachable
configurations of a small instance, projects them onto one or two processes,
and generalizes the result to a quantified candidate invariant, which is then
checked for all sizes, for a restricted class of systems by a small-model
argument.

SMT-based methods automate the search for invariants with satisfiability
modulo theories solvers~\cite{BSST09}. In \emph{array-based
systems}~\cite{GR10,GS10}, the state is a collection of arrays indexed by
process identifiers, and backward reachability is carried out on existentially
quantified formulas; universal guards are handled by an approximation closely
related to monotonic abstraction. The Cubicle model checker~\cite{CGKMZ12}
follows the same principles and uses finite instances to guess
invariants~\cite{CGKMZ13}. The IC3/PDR algorithm~\cite{Bra11,EMB11} has been
extended to infer universally quantified invariants~\cite{KBIRS17,GSM16}. The
Ivy tool~\cite{PMPSS16} restricts verification conditions to a decidable
fragment of first-order logic, which yields small counterexamples to induction
that the user generalizes interactively, and complex consensus protocols such
as Paxos have been verified within this fragment~\cite{PLSS17}. The inference
of quantified invariants for distributed protocols has also been automated,
for instance by generalizing invariants of small instances~\cite{MGJKKS19} or
by searching for quantified separators~\cite{KPIA20}. For fault-tolerant
distributed algorithms, in which processes wait for messages from a number of
other processes that depends on the parameters, \emph{threshold automata} and
a short counterexample property reduce parameterized verification to bounded
model checking with an SMT solver~\cite{JKSVW13,KVW17,KLVW17}.

\subsection{Weak Memory Models}

Parameterized verification is also relevant for concurrent programs running
under weak memory models, where hardware reordering of memory operations is
described by unbounded buffers associated with the
threads~\cite{OSS09,SSONM10,AMT14}. Synchronization primitives, such as locks
and mutual exclusion protocols, are written to be used by any number of
threads, and their correctness must be established for all numbers of threads
under the memory model of the architecture. For a fixed number of threads,
safety verification under Total Store Order (TSO) is decidable by a reduction
to lossy channel systems~\cite{ABBM10}. A \emph{dual} semantics, in which
threads read from load buffers instead of writing to store buffers, makes the
system monotonic with respect to a WQO, which yields the first decidability
result for parameterized verification under TSO through backward
reachability~\cite{AABN16,AABN18}. The problem was later shown to be
\textsc{PSpace}-complete~\cite{AAR20}, and parameterized verification is also
\textsc{PSpace}-complete under the release-acquire semantics of
C11~\cite{KGMC22}. For the richer Intel x86 model, which includes
non-temporal writes~\cite{RMV22,AABKS24}, parameterized verification requires
\emph{better quasi-orderings}~\cite{AABKS26,Mar94}, which had earlier been used
in verification for timed Petri nets~\cite{AN00,Abd10}. Verification under weak
memory models is the subject of Chapter~\chapref{Weak Memory Models}.

\subsection{Parameterized Quantum Circuits}

Parameterized verification has recently been applied to quantum programs, in
which the parameter is the number of qubits. Most quantum algorithms, such as
Shor's factoring algorithm~\cite{Sho97}, Grover's search~\cite{Gro96}, and the
quantum Fourier transform, are described by programs that generate a circuit
for each input size~$n$, and their correctness is a relational property that
relates input states to output states for all~$n$. The framework
of~\cite{AutoQPara} represents infinite families of quantum states by
\emph{synchronized weighted tree automata}, in which an $n$-qubit state is a
binary tree of depth~$n$ whose leaves carry complex amplitudes. Quantum gates
are represented by transducers, which are composed to obtain a transducer for
the whole parameterized program, in analogy with the transitive closure of
transducers in regular model checking (Section~\ref{sec:rmc}). Verification
reduces to functional inclusion and equivalence of these automata, which are
decidable, and the framework gives the first fully automatic verification of
parameterized quantum programs such as the Bernstein-Vazirani algorithm, a
ripple-carry adder, and optimized implementations of Grover's search. It builds
on automata-based verification of quantum circuits of fixed
size~\cite{CCLLTY23,CCLLT23,LSTA25,AutoQ20}. Deductive approaches, based on
quantum Hoare logic~\cite{Yin11}, on deductive verification
frameworks~\cite{Qbricks}, on proof assistants~\cite{HRHWH21}, or on path
sums~\cite{Amy19}, are complementary. Quantum systems are the
subject of Chapter~\chapref{Quantum Systems}.
