\section{Conclusion and Open Problems}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Parameterized verification asks for guarantees that hold for every number of
components, a question that is undecidable in general. The techniques presented
in this chapter respond to this difficulty in different ways, but the same few
ideas recur across all of them: a finite symbolic representation of infinite
sets of configurations, monotonicity with respect to a well-quasi-ordering, and
the observation that the behavior of a large instance is often already visible
in a small one. The protocol of Section~\ref{sec:monotonic} illustrates how
these ideas meet: monotonic abstraction, going backwards from the bad
configurations, and view abstraction, going forwards from the initial ones,
arrive at the same invariant, which is described by three small
configurations.

Several problems remain open or only partially solved. Liveness properties
under fairness are much less well supported than safety properties, both in
theory and in tools, since abstractions that preserve safety often lose the
information needed for fairness. Many protocols have invariants that are not
downward-closed, and finding good representations for such invariants, as
attempted by context-sensitive views, remains a challenge. For weak memory
models, parameterized verification is well understood for TSO and some of its
extensions, but the more relaxed models of ARM and POWER processors, and the
language-level models of C11 and Java, are only partly covered. For distributed
protocols, the automatic inference of invariants with alternating quantifiers,
which are needed for many consensus protocols, remains difficult. In the
quantum setting, the automata-based approach covers parameterized circuits, and
its extension to programs with measurement, classical control, and noise is a
natural next step.
