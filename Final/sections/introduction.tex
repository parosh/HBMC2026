\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The behavior of many computer systems is described by one or more
\emph{parameters}, each of which ranges over a given domain. The parameters may
relate to the size or topology of a network, to the data types over which a
system is built, or to the initial values of variables. The
\emph{parameterized verification problem} is to prove or refute that a
specification holds for all values of the parameters. In this chapter, we
concentrate on a particular class of parameterized systems, namely systems that
consist of an arbitrary number of components, or processes. The size of the
system is then a parameter of the verification problem, and parameterized
verification amounts to proving correctness regardless of the number of
processes.

Parameterized systems appear naturally in many applications, such as mutual
exclusion algorithms, bus protocols, network protocols, cache coherence
protocols, distributed algorithms, and web services. A mutual exclusion
protocol must guarantee that no two processes are in their critical sections at
the same time, whatever the number of processes that participate in a session
of the protocol. A cache coherence protocol must keep the shared data
consistent for any number of caches. Sensor networks may consist of thousands
of nodes, and network protocols may involve millions of participants. Classical
model checking verifies one instance of such a system at a time, for a fixed
number of processes. Even if each process is finite-state, the family of all
instances has an infinite state space, so there is a need to extend
verification from individual instances to the parameterized case.

Parameterized verification is undecidable in general~\cite{AK86,Suz88}, even
when each process is finite-state. Research in the area has therefore
identified classes of systems for which the problem is decidable, developed
algorithms that are not guaranteed to terminate but that often terminate in
practice, and used abstractions that over-approximate the behavior of the
system. A recurring theme is the need for a finite symbolic representation of
infinite sets of configurations, such as regular languages or upward-closed
sets. Another recurring theme is \emph{monotonicity}: the observation that
adding processes to a system often only enables more behavior, which is
exploited by cutoff arguments and by the theory of well-structured transition
systems. A third theme is the observation that the behavior of a large instance
is often already visible in a small one. \emph{Cutoff} methods make this
precise: a static cutoff theorem shows that, for a given class of systems,
correctness for all instances up to a fixed size implies correctness for all
sizes~\cite{EN95,EK00}, while dynamic cutoff methods discover such a size
during the analysis~\cite{AHH16,KKW10}.

This chapter is a revision of the corresponding chapter in the first edition
of this handbook~\cite{AST18}. Instead of surveying all approaches at the same
level of detail, we present three techniques in depth, with their algorithms
and worked examples, and give an overview of the other approaches. The chapter
is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:prelim} introduces parameterized
systems and the reachability problem. Section~\ref{sec:early}
describes the early developments of the field. Section~\ref{sec:rmc} presents
\emph{regular model checking}~\cite{KMMPS97,KW01,BJNT00}, which represents
configurations as words and transition relations as finite-state transducers,
and describes an acceleration technique for computing transitive closures of
transducers. Section~\ref{sec:monotonic} recalls the basic notions of
well-quasi-orderings and backward reachability, and presents \emph{monotonic
abstraction}~\cite{ADHR07}, which over-approximates systems with global
conditions by well-structured transition systems, so that safety can be checked
by backward reachability analysis. Section~\ref{sec:views} presents \emph{view
abstraction}~\cite{AHH13,AHH16}, a forward analysis that inspects only small
instances of the system and detects a cutoff during the analysis.
Section~\ref{sec:other} relates these techniques to other approaches, namely
cutoff theorems, abstraction and compositional reasoning, and deductive and
SMT-based methods, and to other chapters of this handbook; it also discusses
two recent applications, namely programs running under weak memory models and
parameterized quantum circuits. Section~\ref{sec:industrial} discusses
industrial applications, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes with open
problems.
Throughout, we concentrate on safety properties, and mention liveness
properties only occasionally.
